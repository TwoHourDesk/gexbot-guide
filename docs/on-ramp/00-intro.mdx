# The two seats

Everything in this book reads a trade from outside: someone bought, someone else now carries the risk, and the futures tape shows the carrying. One day you may be the one who bought. This page says what changes when you are, in the plainest words available, because the precise words arrive one chapter at a time.

**What this page assumes.** The [reading contract](/docs/intro): the labels, the four pressure words, and the promise that no word is used before it is taught. **What you will be able to do.** Say which seat a sentence is written from, and read the on-ramp page after each chapter knowing what it is for and what it is not.

## Two seats on one ticket

Every trade in the options market has two sides. On one side sits the customer who wanted the trade. On the other sits a firm that took the trade because taking trades is its business, and now has to manage the risk it was handed. The intro gave those two sides their pressure words: the customer has **incentive** — a reason to act, and the freedom not to — and the firm has **mandate** — a policy that makes it act. This book calls the side a sentence is written from the **seat**.

<TermFirst id="seat" />

The main chapters read from outside both seats and infer the dealer seat: what the firm was handed, what it must do, where that lands on ES or NQ. The on-ramp pages sit you in the customer seat and ask the same questions about your own position. The leftover is the same leftover. The pressure word is not: a main-chapter sentence about that leftover may say **mandate**; the on-ramp sentence about the same leftover says **incentive**. That swap is the seat test. If a page uses the other word, it has changed seats without saying so.

## What the on-ramp pages are

One follows each chapter. It obeys every rule the chapter obeys: stake first, teach before naming, a glossary row for every term, a check at the end. It does four things. It retells the chapter’s object with you as the customer. It says what the chapter’s screen tells you about your own position and what it cannot. It names the one thing that changes for you and never changes for a futures trader. It points forward to [Chapter 14](/docs/practice/14-futures-to-options), where the road these pages walk arrives.

It does not recommend, price, or size anything, because the words needed to do that honestly are not yet taught, and because this book does not give entries.

The main chapters never depend on these pages. Skip every one of them and the reading course is intact. Read them and each chapter’s object has a second side.

## What changes, said once

A futures position can be wrong one way: price. The contract you will meet can be wrong three ways, and two of them move while price stands still. Chapter 4 gives the second its name; Chapter 7 gives the third. Chapter 14 calls it “one dimension becomes three,” and everything on its ramp exists so that those two silent ways are obvious to you before you hold the position rather than after.

## Summary

These pages are skippable because Gexbot can be read from outside the ticket. They exist because two of the three ways a position can be wrong never appear on the ES or NQ chart the reader already sits. Skipping them costs nothing as a reader. Holding without them costs the two silent ways.

## Newcomer questions

1. **A leftover sentence says “the customer must sell the calls.” Which seat wrote it, and what is wrong?** A confused seat. “Must” is legal only for the dealer. The customer word is incentive, and “must” is never theirs.
2. **Why not one chapter on options at the end?** There is one — Chapter 14. Each object changes seat differently, and a change is easier to feel next to the object than in a list at the end.
3. **Will these pages tell me which option to buy?** No. Nothing in this book does.
