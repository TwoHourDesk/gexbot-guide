# How to use this book

You can see every print on the ES or NQ tape. You cannot see which of those prints were forced by option positions on the same index, or who was forced to make them. This book is about that second layer. You do **not** need to have traded an option to read it. This page is the contract with you as a reader, not a Gexbot screen.

**What you will be able to do after this page.** Open Chapter 1 knowing what kind of book this is, what it will not try to do, and how this book marks a fact versus a guess.

## What this book is

Gexbot is a set of screens that look at **index options** — contracts tied to the same world as ES and NQ — and draw pictures of leftover positioning. You sit those screens **beside** your futures chart. They do not replace the tape.

This book teaches you to **read** those pictures. It is not a trading system. It will not give you a hit rate, a robot, or a dollar profit target. When later chapters walk through “heuristics,” those are naming drills: practice saying what you are looking at, not orders to send.

There is no options pre-course. When a screen needs a schoolbook word (call, put, strike, and so on), that chapter will stop, define the word in ordinary language, and then return to the Gexbot object. If you already know the word, you can collapse that box and keep walking.

## How this book marks claims

Some sentences here come from Gexbot’s help pages, which this book has not measured. Some are stories traders repeat about what a picture means. Some are ordinary market facts nobody at Gexbot invented. If those three looked alike on the page you would end up treating a story as a mechanism, so each carries a label.

## Four words you will need later

Later chapters ask, for each picture: *who has to do something?* They will use four pressure words. You do not need to apply them yet. You only need to know they exist, and that this book is strict about “must.”

- **Mandate** — a professional options book is run so that a move in the index does not leave them accidentally long or short. When that policy makes them trade the index, this book may say **forced**. Chapter 3 teaches this.
- **Incentive** — the customer who still holds the leftover options is not obliged to do anything. Theta, fear, and profit-and-loss can push them. Write **incentivized**, never forced.
- **None** — buys and sells cancelled. Nobody is left holding the bag. A quiet picture can mean “everything matched,” not “nothing traded.”
- **Unknown** — you can see *where* a pile sits and still not know *who* owns it. Classic, in the next chapter, lives here.

The book is strict about “must” because mandate is the only pressure that does not depend on anyone changing their mind; it is the one you can lean on when the tape moves. Never write “dealers must buy” from a picture that cannot show the owner. Write “if the usual shortcut about ownership holds, dealers would buy.”

## How to read a chapter

Each chapter opens with which Gexbot product you are sitting, what you will be able to read, and what you already need. It closes with a summary that connects the sections, the terms just introduced, a few newcomer questions that stage a case the body did not, a check that makes you produce a combination, and a gate to the next chapter.

Definition boxes in the page match the [glossary](/docs/reference/glossary). The box is the portable definition. The paragraph beside it is the worked case. They are not the same sentences.

## Summary

The labels and the four pressure words do the same job: they stop a story from looking like a mechanism. “Must” is the pressure version of that rule. A sentence without a label and a sentence that says “dealers must” from an unsigned pile fail the same way — they let a guess spend like a fact.

## Newcomer questions

1. **Is this going to tell me when to buy or sell?** No. It teaches you to read a second picture next to your chart. When to act stays with the tape and with you.
2. **Do I need to know options first?** You need an ES or NQ chart. Options words are defined when a screen first needs them.
3. **Why does every other sentence carry a label?** So a sentence from Gexbot’s docs, a common story, and a guess do not look like the same kind of fact.
