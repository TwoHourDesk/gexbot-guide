# Classification and the hedge chain

When a customer lifts 500 calls, someone on the other side now owes the market about 500 ES of buying they did not choose, and possibly 300 more if price keeps rising. That obligation is on no tape. This chapter is how it becomes inferable.

**Which Gexbot capability this is.** The leftover-and-label machinery that Classic does not use. After this chapter you can sit State and Orderflow honestly.

**What you will be able to do.** Sign one option trade as customer bought or customer sold, say what remains after matched trades cancel, walk six links from that trade to a possible futures hedge, and write one pressure sentence for both branches of the running example.

**What you already need.** [Classic](/docs/plans/02-classic): the unsigned map, call versus put, long versus short gamma, and the fact that the same Classic bar can be a buy or a sell. You still do not need to have sent an options order.

## Who crossed the price

An option, like a future, has two posted prices. The **bid** is what a resting buyer will pay. The **ask** (also called the offer) is what a resting seller will take. The gap between them is the **spread**.

Someone has to step across that gap. If they pay the ask, they **lifted the offer** — they bought. If they sell at the bid, they **hit the bid** — they sold. This book calls that person the **aggressor**.

Gexbot watches those crossings during U.S. cash regular hours and asks one narrow question: did the aggressor **buy** the option or **sell** it?

It calls an aggressive buy **customer long**. It calls an aggressive sell **customer short**. “Customer” here is a label, not “a retail trader” and not “they just put on a new long.” Buying to close a short looks the same as buying to open a long. That limit is written in Gexbot’s own docs.

The person (or firm) on the other side of that crossing often **absorbs** the trade: they now hold the mirror. This book’s shorthand for the professional population that usually stands in the middle is **dealer**. A dealer is not one desk.

## Long the option versus short the option

If you **paid** the premium for a call or a put, you are **long the option**. You are long gamma. You want a larger move than the ticket priced.

If you **collected** the premium, you are **short the option**. You are short gamma. You want a smaller move than the ticket priced.

Customer long on Gexbot means they *bought* the option (paid). Customer short means they *sold* it (collected). The next screens draw that sign. Classic cannot.

## What remains after matched trades cancel

Chapter 1 already named the idea: after matched buys and sells of the *same* contract cancel, a **leftover** remains. Two-way trading can be huge while the leftover is tiny.

Gexbot’s name for that leftover is **residual imbalance**, or residual for short. State, Orderflow, and Quant all draw *this* leftover, not yesterday’s open interest and not raw volume.

If two customers cross — one buys, one sells, same contract — the leftover **cancels**. Nobody is left holding the bag. This book’s pressure word is **none**. A quiet later picture can mean “everything matched,” not “nothing traded.”

## Two books

Think of two ledgers that move together.

On the **customer** book: someone just bought or sold paper. Gexbot reports that sign.

On the **dealer** book: someone absorbed that paper and may now need to offset the index risk. Gexbot does not plot a dealer inventory file. You infer the mirror.

<GexbotConcept id="two-books" />
<WhoMustAct id="two-books" />

If the leftover is zero, the second book has nothing new to hedge from *this* tape. Pressure: **none**.

## How stock-like is the leftover? (delta)

To talk about a hedge you need one schoolbook number Classic did not need.

**Delta** is how much the option’s value moves if the index moves one point. A 0.50-delta call behaves like *half* a share of the index, times the contract’s multiplier.

Size times delta times multiplier is the **share-equivalent**: how many “shares of index” the leftover represents. That is what a professional typically offsets in ES, NQ, or SPY. You will see this number drawn as a ladder in Chapter 4. Here you only need it for the running example.

## Who has to act?

The intro previewed four pressure words. Here they become working tools.

A professional options book is usually run so that a move in the index does not leave them accidentally long or short. Their policy and their risk limits require it (*market-general*). The schoolbook name is **delta-neutral**. When that policy makes them trade the index, this book may say **forced**, and the pressure word is **mandate**. “Must” is legal only here, because this is the one pressure that does not depend on anyone changing their mind.

The customer who still holds the leftover is **not** obliged to do anything. Time decay (theta), a falling or rising options market, margin, and profit-and-loss can push them. Write **incentivized**. The pressure word is **incentive**. Never write “the customer must dump.”

**None** you already have: leftover cancelled.

**Unknown** you already have: Classic. Location real, owner not visible.

Professionals do not usually hedge every tick. They hedge when their own risk **bands** are hit. A later ladder can give direction and location. It cannot give the second or the exact size of the hedge. *Market-general.*

<TermFirst id="hedge-in-bands" />

A **hedge** itself is a trade in the index or the futures meant to offset option risk. Gexbot never prints “this ES lot is the hedge.” That sentence is always **inferred**.

## The hedge chain

One leftover print can start a chain. This book uses six links. Every later screen points at a link — and at what that screen cannot show.

1. **Print** — the option trade crossed the spread. An Orderflow bar (Chapter 5) *is* this moment.
2. **Absorb** — the other side now holds the mirror. Often a dealer; sometimes another customer (then leftover cancels).
3. **Initial delta hedge** — the absorber offsets the share-equivalent in the index or futures, *now*. By the time Chapter 4’s DEX ladder exists, this hedge is mostly **already done**.
4. **Gamma re-hedge** — if the index walks, delta changes, and they must top up or unwind. This is **pending** and **path-conditional**: it appears only if the index walks a certain way. Chapter 4’s convexity picture lives here.
5. **Clock re-hedge** — even if the index sits still, time and implied volatility change delta. Chapter 7’s late pictures live here.
6. **Resolution** — at expiry the contract dies. Settlement style matters (Chapter 7). Gexbot does not show this link directly.

You now have the chain. Chapter 4 will show which link each State drawing is. What is left for this chapter is which way each link pushes.

Liquidity roles (*inferred*, never a labeled futures print):

- A book that is **long** gamma hedges *against* the move: sell rips, buy dips. This book says **fade**. That supplies liquidity. The usual calm / **pin** story lives here — a hypothesis, not a fact.
- A book that is **short** gamma hedges *with* the move: buy rips, sell dips. This book says **chase**. That takes liquidity. Moves can get larger.
- Do not invert that polarity. Your futures reflex says buying is bullish. The short-gamma book that buys into the rip is not expressing a view; it is being pulled up by its own position, and it would rather not be. That is what **chase** means here.

## Running example — both branches

10:05 Eastern. A customer trades **500 SPX 0DTE ATM calls**. Delta about **0.50**.

Share-equivalent: 500 × 0.50 × $100 per point = $25,000 per index point. One ES future is $50 per point, so that is about **500 ES**. Those dollar multipliers are *market-general*, not a Gexbot invention. Chapter 6 will sit the conversion properly.

**Bought branch.** The customer is long the calls (paid). The dealer absorbed: short 500 calls, short gamma. Initial hedge (link 3): the dealer **buys** about 500 ES now — **done** by the time a later DEX bar exists. If the index then rises 15 points and delta walks toward 0.80 (a toy number, not a live Gexbot print), the dealer buys about 300 more ES **into strength**. That is link 4: gamma re-hedge, pending, path-conditional. Pressure: dealer **mandate** to **chase**.

**Sold branch.** The customer sold the same 500 calls (collected). The dealer is long those calls, long gamma. Initial hedge: **sells** about 500 ES now. Into the same rip, sells about 300 more — **fades**. Pressure: dealer **mandate** to fade.

**Same Classic bar, opposite forced re-hedge.** That sentence is why Classic is not State.

If two customers had crossed the same 500 calls, leftover would be zero. Link 3 does not happen. Pressure: **none**.

## One sentence for who must act

This book’s **forced-flow sentence** is one line:

> Actor · pressure · trigger · direction · venue — or the word **unknown**.

Classic wall on this print: **unknown**.

Bought-branch leftover, pending gamma: **dealer · mandate · index up · buy ES (chase) · ES**. The customer on that leftover: **customer · incentive · approach to the strike · may sell the option (*folklore*)**.

<TermFirst id="forced-flow-sentence" />

“Forced” is only mandate. Everything else is incentivized, none, or unknown.

## Summary

Classification signs leftover paper as customer bought versus customer sold. After matched trades cancel, only the residual remains. The hedge chain maps each later screen to a link, and to what that screen cannot show. Classic still cannot split the two branches you just wrote.

## Newcomer questions

1. **Did they just put on longs?** Not stated. Buy to close looks the same as buy to open.
2. **Is the dealer about to buy because leftover delta printed?** The initial hedge is mostly already done. Pending force lives on gamma (Chapter 4) and on the clock (Chapter 7).
3. **State is nearly empty but my tape says volume is heavy. Which one is wrong?** Neither. Residual can be tiny while two-way volume is huge. Pressure: none.
