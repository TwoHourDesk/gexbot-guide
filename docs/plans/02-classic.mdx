# Classic

**Which Gexbot screen this is.** Classic — the unsigned gamma-exposure map from [Chapter 1](/docs/plans/01-what-gexbot-is).

**What you will be able to do.** Read left and right on the histogram, say what last night’s leftover contracts add versus today’s trading, name the tallest pile and the fulcrum, and write the pressure word **unknown** every time.

<TermFirst id="unknown-pressure" />

**What you already need.** Chapter 1’s meanings of GEX, unsigned, open interest, and volume. You still do not need to have traded an option. This chapter teaches the contract words the histogram is drawn from.

## Words the histogram is made of

Classic’s bars are built from a handful of contract words. A reader without them sees a shape where there is a count.

Classic is a **histogram**: a stack of bars, one bar per **strike**. A strike is the price level written on the option contract — the shelf in the warehouse photo.

There are two kinds of option. A **call** is the right to *buy* the index at that strike. A **put** is the right to *sell* the index at that strike. The buyer of either contract pays a **premium** (the ticket price). The seller collects it.

The contract dies at **expiry**. If that expiry is *today*, this book says **0DTE** (zero days to expiry). Those contracts own most of the fast-changing sensitivity you will meet as **gamma** in a moment. That is why 0DTE bars dominate Classic.

Where the strike sits versus the index is **moneyness**. Near the index: **at the money (ATM)**. A call with strike below the index, or a put with strike above it: **in the money (ITM)**. The other way: **out of the money (OTM)**.

Chapter 1 said the firms in the middle of the options market buy and sell the index as it moves. How much index they need to hold against an option is not fixed; it changes as the index moves, and **gamma** is how fast it changes per point. Think of it not as the car’s speed but as how sharply the accelerator itself gets more or less sensitive as you press it. Long calls and long puts are long gamma; short calls and short puts are short gamma. Gamma has no up or down. It peaks ATM and grows sharply into expiry, which is why a cheap 0DTE can be a huge gamma pile while an expensive far-dated ticket is not. The ticket price is the **debit** — what the buyer paid. Gexbot weights by gamma, not by debit.

As the clock runs, an option’s value tends to leak even if the index does not move. The schoolbook name is **theta**, or time decay. Classic does not plot theta. You will need the word in Chapter 4 when customer incentive appears.

U.S. cash **regular trading hours (RTH)** for this book are 09:30–16:00 Eastern. Classic’s open-interest map is mostly last night’s official snapshot. Its volume map rewrites during the cash session.

The cash index this running example uses is **SPX** (the S&P 500). Chapter 6 will convert SPX onto ES.

## GEX from last night’s leftover contracts

Take gamma at a strike, multiply by how many contracts are still open from the official snapshot, net calls against puts. That is **GEX by open interest**. It is last night’s topographic map. The hills are still there at 09:31 even if nobody has walked them today.

## Call versus put at one strike

At each shelf, Classic subtracts put GEX from call GEX. One bar. Right means the call side won the tug of war. Left means the put side won. Two huge equal piles can net toward zero. That is a **balanced node**, not an empty warehouse.

## The fulcrum Classic calls zero gamma

**Zero gamma** is the weighted center of today’s volume-GEX complex. It is not necessarily a traded strike. Spot can sit on it without anyone having that strike as a wall.

A common story says: below this fulcrum the book is more short-gamma (moves get larger); above it, more long-gamma (moves get pinned). That story **assumes** a usual ownership shortcut — the same one most public GEX commentary assumes. Classic cannot check it, and if the owner is the other way round the story runs backwards. If you use the shortcut, write *if*.

## Tallest hill, deepest hole

**Major positive** and **major negative** are the largest right bar and the largest left bar on that unsigned map. They jump when a bigger node prints or when the index moves the gamma surface. They are candidates people call magnets or walls. Whether a hole is a fade or an acceleration lane depends on ownership — unknown here.

## Where the map just redrew

**Max-change** strikes are the shelves whose GEX changed the most over the last 1, 5, 15, or 30 minutes. Location of fresh curvature. Not a direction.

**Lookback** dots show the same histogram at earlier clocks today. The slider replays the day. A pile that *built* into you is a different object from a pile you inherited at 09:31. Dots are not a forecast.

## Running example (10:05 Eastern)

A customer trades **500 SPX 0DTE ATM calls**. Classic’s volume-GEX bar at that strike grows **to the right** (call side).

Now the mirror: the customer **sold** the same 500 calls. Classic’s bar still grows **to the right**. **The bar is identical.** Owner unknown. Pressure unknown.

That is why Classic is not State. You now have a location. Chapter 3 adds the sign — who bought and who sold — and with it the direction of the hedge.

## Summary

The running example’s identical bars are why every Who-must-act row on this page says **unknown**. Location survived the buy/sell split. The hedge sentence did not. A reader who writes a hedge from Classic has used a later chapter’s object on a screen that cannot hold it.

## Newcomer questions

1. **Is a big green bar bullish?** It is call-sided gamma. On SPX, Gexbot’s own docs often treat that as *sold* calls (*folklore*). Whether it stalls or fuels depends on owner and on whether implied vol is falling or rising. Classic cannot show the owner.
2. **Call pile and put pile at one strike are huge and equal.** Is the warehouse empty? No. That is a **balanced node**. Classic’s one bar nets toward zero; both piles are still there.
